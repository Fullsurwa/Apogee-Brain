# AI Governance Investigation — Working One-Pager

## Purpose
A practical investigation method for understanding a real-world AI governance problem before reaching conclusions or selecting controls.

**Learn → Question → Test → Decide → Build**

## 1. Understand the Situation
Establish:
- What is happening?
- What AI system, process, decision, or capability is involved?
- Where does AI enter the process?
- Who is involved and who is affected?
- What outcome or concern has actually been observed?
- What does the organisation believe the problem is?

Do not assume the initial description is the complete problem.

## 2. Separate the Starting Information
Distinguish:
- **Facts** — what is established
- **Claims** — what someone says is true
- **Assumptions** — what is being taken for granted
- **Evidence** — what supports a claim
- **Unknowns** — what is not established
- **Questions** — what needs investigation

## 3. Investigate
Turn the initial situation into specific questions.

### AI system
- What does the system actually do?
- What data does it use?
- What decisions or outputs does it influence?
- What happens to its output afterwards?

### People and impact
- Who benefits?
- Who may be harmed?
- Who decides?
- Who can challenge the outcome?
- What happens if the system is wrong?
- At what scale could the effects occur?

### Evidence
- What evidence exists?
- Where did it come from?
- How reliable is it?
- What does it establish?
- What does it not establish?
- What information is missing?

## 4. Apply Relevant Governance Knowledge
Use the sources appropriate to the situation:
- laws and regulations
- official guidance
- standards
- organisational policies
- academic research
- technical documentation
- documented incidents
- ethical frameworks
- sector-specific guidance

A framework or principle is not automatically applicable. Ask what it actually tells us about this particular situation.

## 5. Define the Specific Problem
Move from a broad concern to a precise question.

For example:

**“The AI may be unfair.”**

becomes:

- What does “fair” mean here?
- Which groups are being compared?
- What outcome is being measured?
- How is it being measured?
- Could other factors explain the difference?
- What evidence supports the claim?
- What remains unknown?

**A principle is not yet a finding.**

## 6. Evaluate Evidence
For important conclusions, distinguish:

**What we know**  
**What we believe**  
**What the evidence supports**  
**What remains uncertain**

Research informs the conclusion; it does not determine the conclusion automatically.

Questions should become more specific as understanding increases.

## 7. Identify Possible Controls
Only after the problem is sufficiently understood consider responses.

**Principle → specific problem → definition → measurement → evidence → possible control → evaluation**

Controls may be:
- technical
- organisational
- procedural
- contractual
- governance-based
- human oversight
- monitoring
- validation
- documentation
- correction/redress mechanisms

The appropriate response depends on the actual risk and context.

## 8. Test the Proposed Response
Ask:
- Does the control address the identified problem?
- What evidence would show that it works?
- What new risks could it introduce?
- What trade-offs exist?
- What assumptions does it depend on?
- What happens when it fails?
- How will it be monitored after deployment?

## 9. State What Is Established

### Established
What the evidence supports.

### Supported interpretation
What the available evidence reasonably suggests.

### Unresolved
What cannot yet be established.

### Further investigation
What evidence or analysis is still required.

### Possible actions
Options that could be considered, with their rationale and trade-offs.

## Investigation Loop

**Understand → Identify facts/claims/assumptions/evidence/unknowns → Question → Investigate → Gather evidence → Refine the problem → Apply relevant governance knowledge → Test possible controls → Evaluate → Establish findings → Identify unknowns → Further investigation/action → Learn and revisit**

## Five Questions to Keep Returning To

**Who benefits?**  
**Who is harmed?**  
**Who decides?**  
**Who can challenge?**  
**At what scale?**

Across the lifecycle:

**Where is responsibility being exercised — design, deployment, use, monitoring, correction, or redesign?**

## Working Principle

**Understand first. Investigate second. Conclude last.**

The purpose is to establish:

**What happened → what we know → what the evidence supports → what we do not know → what needs investigating → what options exist → what should be decided by the responsible human or organisation.**

The framework supports professional judgment; it does not replace it.
